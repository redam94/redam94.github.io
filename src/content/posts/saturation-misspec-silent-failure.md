The [adstock and saturation identification post](/posts/adstock-saturation-identification/) covers the ridge in the joint posterior when you try to estimate both transforms together. There's a companion failure that's arguably worse: using the right model structure but the wrong saturation shape. The harm isn't a wide posterior. It's a confident one, centered in the wrong place, passing every standard check you'd use to catch it.

Here's the quantified result from the structural stress matrix in [`mmm-framework`](https://github.com/redam94/mmm-framework): fitting a strictly concave saturation curve to data generated by an S-shaped response produces 43% median attribution error and 25% coverage — three of four channels with true contributions outside their 90% credible intervals. R-hat: 1.01. Divergences: zero. Posterior predictive check: passes. Causal refutation suite: passes.

Every implemented check is green. The attribution is deeply wrong.

## Two families of saturation curve

Most MMMs assume that media response is strictly concave everywhere: each additional dollar buys less than the last, starting from the very first dollar. The 1-minus-exponential and logistic forms both have this shape.

Real media response isn't always like this. Brand campaigns — TV in particular — often exhibit an S-shaped response: minimal effect at very low exposure (awareness hasn't accumulated yet), steep response in the middle range, then diminishing returns at saturation. The Hill function with $n > 1$ produces this shape:

$$f(x) = \frac{x^n}{\kappa^n + x^n}$$

At $n = 1$ this is strictly concave. At $n = 2$ or $n = 3$ it develops a lower threshold — the response is nearly flat below $\kappa$ and steep above it.

The difference matters enormously for the counterfactual that attribution actually depends on. When you set a channel's spend to zero and ask "how much did sales fall?", you're querying the curve in a region you probably never observed. If the true curve is S-shaped but your model assumes strictly concave, the model misestimates what happens at zero spend — and the attribution error follows directly.

## The mechanism

Say a channel's typical weekly spend sits in the steep middle of the true S-curve — the region where each dollar is genuinely effective. A strictly concave curve fitted to the observed spend range can match those observations well, because the steep middle of an S-curve looks like the steep part of a concave curve locally.

But the extrapolation to zero is completely different. The true S-shaped curve is nearly flat near zero spend — there's a threshold below which awareness doesn't build. The fitted concave curve, by contrast, extrapolates a steep rise starting from zero (concave curves have their maximum marginal return at the origin).

The counterfactual says: "sales would have been much lower at zero spend, because the curve rises steeply from zero." That's what the model believes. The truth says: "sales would have been nearly the same at low spend, because you're below the threshold." The attribution bias is positive — the model systematically over-credits media.

The in-sample fit is fine. Both curves pass through the observed spend range. You'd need to observe the model's behavior at spend levels far outside your data to notice the disagreement — which is exactly what standard diagnostics don't look at.

## What the stress test found

The `saturation_misspec` scenario in `mmm-framework/tests/synth/` generates data from an S-shaped Hill response ($n \approx 2$) and fits the model's default strictly concave saturation. The results from the structural stress matrix (156 weeks, 4 channels, 600/600/4 MCMC):

| channel | true contribution | estimated | relative error | in 90% CI |
|---------|:-----------------:|:---------:|:--------------:|:---------:|
| TV      | 2,658             | 4,397     | +65%           | ✗         |
| Search  | 3,868             | 4,294     | +11%           | ✓         |
| Social  | 3,106             | 4,767     | +53%           | ✗         |
| Display | 2,456             | 3,271     | +33%           | ✗         |

Total media contribution is 38% over-estimated. Three of four channels excluded from their 90% CI. And here's the part I find genuinely alarming: `ppc = ✓`, `refut = ✓`. The posterior predictive check passes because the fitted curve can replicate the observed sales trajectory — the in-sample fit is fine. The causal refutation suite passes because the refuter is working with the same wrong functional form.

Compare this to the `adstock_misspec` scenario — wrong carryover window — where the robustness value flags two channels as fragile and the fit diverges on held-out data. Wrong adstock at least makes some noise. Wrong saturation is quiet.

## Why posterior predictive checks miss this

A posterior predictive check generates replicated datasets from the fitted model and asks whether the observed data looks like a typical sample from those replications. When saturation is misspecified, the model adjusts $\beta$ (the channel coefficient) to compensate — if the curve shape underestimates response in the observed range, the coefficient is inflated to match. The in-sample fit looks good because the product $\beta \cdot f(x_t^{\text{adstock}})$ fits the data regardless of which functional form $f$ is.

The PPC tests the product. It can't decompose the product into "is $\beta$ too high because the curve shape is wrong?" That decomposition requires observing the system at spend levels it was never at — either below the threshold or well above the observed range.

This is why [Simulation-Based Calibration](/posts/simulation-based-calibration/) belongs in the workflow even after PPC. SBC generates data from the model's own prior-predictive distribution and checks whether the posterior recovers those parameters. If your prior puts weight on both concave and S-shaped scenarios, an SBC run that included S-shaped ground truth would catch the miscalibration. But SBC checks the model against its own generative story — not against a different functional form.

There is no standard diagnostic that checks whether your assumed functional form matches reality. That requires external evidence.

## What actually catches it

The [`mmm-framework` calibration module](https://github.com/redam94/mmm-framework) exists for exactly this. A geo-lift or incrementality experiment gives you a measured media effect at a specific spend level — a point on the true response curve, with its associated uncertainty. When that measured lift disagrees with the model's posterior predictive attribution, one of them is wrong, and the experiment has a much stronger claim to being right.

In the saturation misspecification scenario, the fitted model over-attributes media. A well-designed holdout test — running at a spend level in the region where the S-curve and concave curve diverge most — would show a smaller lift than the model predicted. That gap is the diagnostic. The experiment doesn't care about your functional form assumption.

The practical implication: if your attribution is based on observational data alone and you haven't tested what happens when you significantly cut a channel's spend, you don't know which saturation family you're in. The default assumption of strict concavity might be right for direct-response digital channels with immediate purchase paths. It's much more questionable for brand channels with threshold effects.

This is also a reason to be cautious about the specific shape parameters your model reports. A half-saturation constant $\kappa$ and a shape parameter $n$ are weakly identified even when the functional form is correct — as the [equifinality note in `mmm-framework`](https://github.com/redam94/mmm-framework/blob/main/technical-docs/methodology/equifinality.md) documents. If the functional form is wrong, these parameters are fitting noise to a misspecified curve, and reporting them as if they characterized the true response adds a second layer of false confidence on top of the first.

## Practical implications

Three things follow from this.

**Vary your spend.** Saturation misspecification matters most when your observed spend range never tests the boundary of the curve. If you've always spent in the same range, you have no data that distinguishes concave from S-shaped response. Deliberate spend variation — including periods well below and well above typical levels — produces observations that constrain the functional form.

**Run shutoff tests.** A channel set to near-zero spend for a period directly tests the low-spend region of the curve. Whether sales drop immediately (concave, threshold-less) or stay flat (S-shaped, below threshold) tells you which family you're in. This is the cheapest saturation diagnostic available. Planned dark periods also help identify adstock; they're doing double duty.

**Don't trust functional form tightly.** For channels where you suspect threshold effects — brand TV, out-of-home, upper-funnel programmatic — fit both concave and S-shaped alternatives and compare against held-out lift tests. If both produce similar in-sample fit but different counterfactual predictions, the functional form uncertainty is material and should be reported as such, not averaged away.

The stress matrix finding is a specific, repeatable result: fitting a strictly concave saturation to an S-shaped truth produces over-attribution in the 40-65% range per channel, with full MCMC convergence and passing PPC and refutation. The problem is invisible from inside the model. The fix is external evidence — experiments that force the model to predict spend levels it hasn't seen, and compare those predictions to what actually happens.
