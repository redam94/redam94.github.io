The "100-year flood" is the flow with a 1% chance of being exceeded in any one year. It is the line on the flood map, and it gets misread in two ways. The first is "once a century." The second is quieter and does more damage: people read it as a fixed fact about the river.

It is an estimate: a 0.99 quantile fitted to about a century of annual maxima, a sample in which the observations that matter most are the rarest by construction. I built a [worked example](https://redam94.github.io/pymc-challenges/examples/E113_hundred_year_flood.html) around the Susquehanna at Harrisburg to see how much that matters. Depending on which defensible information went into the model, the chance of beating the nominal 100-year flow during a 30-year mortgage ran from about 29% down to about 8%. The interval on a return level is the result. So is the list of assumptions that moves it.

## The arithmetic of a mortgage

Start with the part that needs no model. A flow with a 1% annual exceedance chance is beaten at least once in 30 independent years with probability

$$P_{30} = 1 - (1 - 0.01)^{30} \approx 0.26$$

More generally, if $F_t(x)$ is the probability that year $t$'s peak stays below $x$, the chance of at least one exceedance over a horizon is $1 - \prod_t F_t(x)$. That is the quantity a homeowner cares about: a named event, a stated horizon, a probability. "Return period" is a property of a fitted curve. The 26% is a property of a decision.

## One gauge, 134 years, a quarter of a million cfs

The US standard for flood frequency (Bulletin 17C, England et al. 2019) fits a log-Pearson III distribution to the base-10 logs of the annual peaks. It is not the GEV from the extreme-value textbooks, but the inferential problem is the same: three parameters, a mean $\mu$, a spread $\sigma$, and a skew $\gamma$ that sets the upper tail and is the thing a single gauge knows least about.

The one-gauge model in the worked example is short. The drainage area, the log peaks, and the centre and sd of the skew prior are data. The log density `p3_logp` is hand-written there, because the Pearson III support has a wall that moves with every parameter:

```python
import numpy as np
import pymc as pm
import pytensor.tensor as pt

with pm.Model() as one_gauge:
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", 5.0 + 0.8 * np.log10(area / 1e4), 0.5)
    sigma = pm.LogNormal("sigma", np.log(0.18), 0.4)
    z = pm.Normal("z_gamma", 0.0, 1.0)
    gamma = pm.Deterministic("gamma", skew_centre + skew_sd * z)
    pm.Potential("gauged", pt.sum(p3_logp(log10_peaks, mu, sigma, gamma)))
```

Harrisburg has a gapless gauged record of 134 water years. That sounds like plenty until you count how many times a 1-in-100 event is expected to show up in it: 1.34. In the worked example, the posterior for the 100-year flow from the gauged years alone sits near 720,000 cubic feet per second, with an 89% interval of roughly 620,000 to 880,000. A quarter of a million cfs of uncertainty, from more than a century of clean data.

Turning draws into the two quantities of interest takes a few lines of SciPy:

```python
from scipy import stats

def return_level(mu, sigma, gamma, p=0.99):
    """Flow with annual non-exceedance probability p, one value per draw."""
    return 10 ** stats.pearson3.ppf(p, gamma, loc=mu, scale=sigma)

def chance_in_30(x, mu, sigma, gamma):
    """Chance of at least one annual peak above x in 30 years, per draw."""
    logcdf = stats.pearson3.logcdf(np.log10(x), gamma, loc=mu, scale=sigma)
    return -np.expm1(30 * logcdf)
```

Here is the first place the uncertainty stops being a footnote. Take a fixed "textbook" 100-year flow (about 715,000 cfs, computed in the worked example from the gauged years by the older hand method; the official map value may differ) and average `chance_in_30` over the posterior. You get 27% to 29%, not 26%. Exceedance probability is a nonlinear function of the parameters, so averaging over what you do not know gives a different number than plugging in the point estimate. Hydrologists call this the expected probability. The uncertainty does not sit symmetrically around the answer. It moves the answer.

## "Nothing bigger for a century" is data

Harrisburg flooded before it had a gauge. The record names six floods between 1786 and 1889, and it implies something just as useful about the other 98 years: nobody wrote down anything to rival them. Each recorded peak $q_h$ enters the likelihood through its density. Each of the $m$ unrecorded years says only "below the perception threshold $X$," which is a censored observation:

$$\sum_h \log p(q_h) + m \log F(X)$$

Adding those terms moved the 100-year flow down by about 8%, to about 660,000 cfs, and shrank its 89% interval by a third. A century in which nothing came close to the largest gauged flood is evidence against the heavy tail the gauged years alone allowed. One detail is instructive: replacing each old flood's value with a ±25% interval changed almost nothing. The fact of the old marks mattered more than their precision.

## What each piece of information did

The worked example builds the answer one source of information at a time. These are its numbers:

| Information in the model               | 100-year flow, median (cfs) | 30-year chance of beating the textbook flow |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Gauge alone, vague skew prior          | 725,655                     | 29.2%                                       |
| + regional skew prior                  | 714,924                     | 27.4%                                       |
| + floods of 1786 to 1889               | 660,766                     | 18.8%                                       |
| + eleven neighbouring gauges           | 636,793                     | 14.4%                                       |
| + decline since 1950, at today's level | 576,556                     | 8.1%                                        |
| Three trend shapes, stacked            | 604,743                     | 10.8%                                       |

Across the rows the point estimate moves by up to a fifth and the probability by a factor of more than three. No row is wrong given what it conditions on, and no row is "the" answer without saying what it conditions on.

## The uncertainty the posterior does not show

The last two rows depend on a question the standard method assumes away: is the river the same river it was? Bulletin 17C treats the record as stationary, and Milly et al. (2008) argued in _Science_ that stationarity is dead. The usual worry is heavier rain. Here the data lean the other way. Pooled over twelve gauges, the worked example estimates a regional decline of $-0.066 \pm 0.020$ log10 units per century since 1950, with posterior probability above 0.99 that it is negative.

That 0.99 is too sure. It is another case of [more data producing more confidence than it has earned](/posts/more-data-more-confident-still-wrong/): the twelve gauges share their storms. Their yearly log peaks correlate at about 0.56 on average, which makes them worth roughly 1.7 independent gauges for a change they all share. Leave out one peak at a time and the decline model beats the stationary one by about 6 log units with a standard error of 3.5. Leave out a whole water year at a time, all gauges together, and the lead is about 4 with a standard error near 9. Stacking on the year-blocked comparison gives the decline about half the weight and no change about a third.

So the headline is about 11%, with an 89% range from 3% to 22%: about 8% if the decline is real and holds, about 14% if the river has not changed. The data cannot separate dams, regrown forests, and fewer ice jams as causes. That range is the finding.

## What to do in practice

1. **Report the event over the horizon.** "About 11 in 100 over a 30-year mortgage" answers the question people have. "The 100-year flow is 604,743 cfs" answers one they did not ask, to [six figures the model never earned](/posts/false-precision-in-reporting/).

2. **Put the interval in the headline.** A range of 3% to 22% tells the reader how much weight the number can bear.
3. **Show the ladder.** A table of what each information source did is the most honest sensitivity analysis I know. It tells the reader exactly which assumption to argue with.

4. **Check the independence your precision rests on.** If your units share shocks, block the cross-validation by the shared unit and see whether the comparison survives.

5. **Treat stationarity as a model choice.** The worked example holds each trend model at its current level rather than extrapolating the line, and shows the alternatives side by side.

6. **Test the tail machinery before trusting it.** Tails are where numerical code is least exercised. The worked example found a library gradient that was off by orders of magnitude at the shape values these rivers have, and a centred parameterisation that stranded chains at two of five seeds. [Read the diagnostics first](/posts/read-the-diagnostics-first/), per chain.

A return level is the least-observed quantity in the dataset, estimated through the parameter the data constrain least, under an assumption about the future that the data cannot verify. Reporting it as a single number discards most of what the analysis learned.
